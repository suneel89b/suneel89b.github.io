Artifact 4 · Explainer for non-technical teams

Neural Networks, Explained Simply

A short, plain-language explanation of how a neural network learns, written so that people without a technical background can understand the AI tools they are being asked to use.

Individual work. AIML-500: Machine Learning Fundamentals, Communicating for Learning.

The explanation

A neural network is a computer program that learns to recognize patterns by practicing on many examples and correcting its own mistakes.

Think of a new employee

Imagine teaching a new employee to sort satellite images into "water," "forest," or "city." You do not give them a rulebook. You show them thousands of labeled images, let them guess, and tell them when they are wrong. Over time, they get better. A neural network learns the same way.

How it works

  1. Input. The network receives raw data, like the pixels of a satellite image.
  2. Layers of small decision-makers. The data passes through layers of "neurons." Each neuron does a tiny calculation and passes the result on. Early layers notice simple things, like edges and colors. Deeper layers combine those into bigger patterns, like rivers, fields, or city blocks.
  3. A guess. The last layer gives an answer, such as "80% water."
  4. Learning from mistakes. If the guess is wrong, the network works backward and adjusts the strength of the connections between its neurons. This is called backpropagation. After thousands of rounds, the guesses become accurate.

Why "deep"?

A network with many layers is called a deep neural network. More layers let it learn more complex patterns.

Why it matters

In my work with satellite imagery, a person cannot review millions of images manually. A trained neural network can label new images automatically within seconds, so people can focus on checking the results and making decisions.

Check yourself

If the network keeps calling shadows "water," would you change its layers, or show it more labeled examples of shadows? Hint: a network can only learn patterns it has seen.

Description

A one-page explainer that teaches how neural networks learn, using an everyday analogy (training a new employee), four short steps, a visual diagram of the layers, and a real example from satellite imagery. It ends with a quick self-check question so readers can test their understanding.

Objective

To practice the course challenge of explaining a complex idea simply, and to apply communication strategies from the lesson, such as storytelling, chunking, and progressive disclosure, to a technical topic.

Process

I used an AI tool to understand neural networks in more detail and to organize my ideas. The first explanation it produced was too technical for a general audience, so I asked for simpler language, an everyday analogy, and short steps. I then added the satellite imagery example from my own work and wrote the final version in my own words for the class discussion.

In the discussion, a classmate suggested that a diagram of the layers would help readers who think visually. For this portfolio version, I added that diagram, along with a short self-check question so readers can apply what they learned.

Tools & Technologies

  • Claude (Anthropic) for research support and organizing ideas
  • Course lesson on communication strategies for learning (AIML-500)

Value Proposition

This explainer gives IT managers a ready-made way to introduce AI to their teams, so people understand the tools they are asked to trust instead of treating them as a black box.

Unique Value

It explains neural networks without math, using a familiar workplace analogy and a real example from my own field. The diagram shows both how information flows forward and how the network learns backward from its mistakes, which is the part most simple explanations leave out.

Relevance

When an organization introduces AI, the biggest barrier is often understanding, not technology. People are more willing to adopt a tool, and more likely to question it when something looks wrong, when they know how it works. Clear explanations like this one help leaders build that trust during change.
